    How it was tested. Two pilots used 200 generated questions each (textbook, technician shorthand and chat style), with pass lines written down before any request was sent. Pilot 1 failed at 159 of 200: it asked Jev seven separate yes/no questions about what was being asked, and Jev over-answered them on shorthand. Pilot 2 used fresh questions and a new design: return all seven properties, and let the unit written next to a number narrow its options. It scored 194 of 200, which was the pass line. Chat and textbook questions were all right; shorthand was the weak spot (61 of 67, mostly “Tdp” read as dry-bulb). The dropdown next to each number lets you fix a wrong reading in one click.

    Reading uploaded charts. This runs in your browser with Tesseract.js OCR; the image isn’t uploaded. Jev reads the chart’s text to decide the chart type and units, and code lines up the axes and checks them against the equations. On 10 fresh test images of two chart designs, 9 were read within ±0.3 °C and ±0.2 g/kg, which was the pass line. The miss was 13.8 °C off, because its pressure label wasn’t read. An earlier version failed at 8 of 10 on small images. It hasn’t been tested on phone photos, skewed scans or IP-unit charts. Keep the dashed curves switched on to see whether the fit looks right.

    The math. Moist-air relations from the ASHRAE Handbook Fundamentals (2017), chapter 1, the same ones PsychroLib uses. They were checked against the sea-level chart: its RH, enthalpy and volume lines agree to within 0.2% RH, 0.05 kJ/kg and 0.0003 m³/kg. The 0 °C wet-bulb line is off by up to 0.3 °C, because the equations switch from water to ice there. Charts other than sea level are drawn by this page from the same equations.
